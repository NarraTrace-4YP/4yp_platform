A novel application of LLM wikis to the analysis of Domestic Homicide Reviews

Project
Our fourth year group project (CS407) in the Department of Computer Science at the University of Warwick
Client
Forensic Capability Network, the national network for forensic science in policing in England and Wales
Supervisor
Gabriele Pergola

The question

To what extent can an LLM wiki help forensic researchers identify connections and recurring patterns across Domestic Homicide Reviews?

From the project specification

Why it matters

Since 2011, Community Safety Partnerships in England and Wales have had to commission a Domestic Homicide Review when someone aged 16 or over dies through violence, abuse or neglect by a relative, partner or member of their household. The agencies involved look back at what happened to learn how to protect people better, not to decide who was at fault.

Each review covers a single death. Failings that recur only become visible when many reviews are read together, and that reading has mostly been done by hand: one 2023 study gathered 302 documents and could draw findings from 46 of them.

The usual way to put questions to a collection of documents, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), finds the passages that answer a specific question. It is less suited to ideas spread across a whole collection. An LLM wiki works differently: an agent reads each document once and writes what it learns into linked pages, so a connection noted once can be reused. As far as the team knows, this is the first time one has been applied to these reviews.

On 15 September 2026, Domestic Homicide Reviews became Domestic Abuse Related Death Reviews, which also cover suicides and unexplained deaths linked to domestic abuse. Every review this project works with is a Domestic Homicide Review, so its findings may not carry over to the wider set of deaths the new reviews cover.

How it works

Four stages, each working from what the one before produced, with six objectives between them. Stages 2 and 3 are the heart of the research.

  1. 1

    Corpus preparation

    • O1Organise the reviews by their own headings and map their sections to the 2016 report template, in a database linked to each review's case details.
  2. 2

    Knowledge organisation

    • O2Build an LLM wiki over that database, with linked pages on people, agencies, events and recommendations. A separate model checks each claim against its source before the wiki is used.
  3. 3

    Analysis

    • O3Use the wiki to find similar cases, patterns over time, recurring recommendations and lessons, and critical events.
  4. 4

    Evaluation

    • O4Rebuild case narratives from the wiki, with details hidden or swapped, to test what a model understands and where it is biased.
    • O5Run the Stage 3 tasks on the wiki and on a RAG baseline, and compare.
    • O6Find case details the reviews support but the official records leave out.

The analysis tasks

What Stage 3 asks of the wiki. The same four tasks are run on the RAG baseline in Stage 4.

TaskQuestionMethod
Case similaritiesWhich reviews are comparable to a given case, and why?LLM comparison
Patterns over timeWhich sequences of behaviour and agency response recur across cases, and how often?Sequence mining
Recurring recommendationsWhich recommendations and lessons recur, and which agencies do they concern?Codebook and topic model
Critical eventsWhich events in a review support the case details recorded for it?Greedy set cover

The data

DHRC-100
100 reviews converted from PDF into structured files and checked by hand. The project's main dataset.
DHRC-RAW
All 702 reviews gathered, including raw versions of the same 100, as automated output nobody has checked. Used as an unseen test set for Stage 1.
Case metadata
The details the Home Office DHR Library records for each review. Kept apart from the wiki and the retrieval index, and used only to evaluate them.
Guidance
The 2016 statutory guidance, whose report template Stage 1 maps each review onto, and the 2026 guidance that replaced it.

How we work

The project follows Design Science Research: build something, evaluate it, and let what the evaluation finds shape the next version. Work runs in two-week sprints, each ending in a supervisor meeting. About ten reviews go through the whole pipeline before it is run on all 100, so problems show up early.

All code lives in one shared GitHub repository. Every change goes in through a pull request that passes automated tests and is approved by another member. Pipelines run on a self-hosted Windmill server, and the language models run on Warwick's Blythe GPU cluster.

Data and ethics

The reviews are published under the Open Government Licence. They contain personal information, so they are handled under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Data and model inference stay on approved systems rather than outside services, and anything built from the reviews is checked for identifying details before it is released.

Team members do not contact victims' families. Every claim a language model makes is treated as unverified until it has been checked against its source.

Reading these accounts can take a toll. The team takes breaks, checks in with each other, and uses the university's wellbeing services when needed.
