Exploring patterns across Domestic Homicide Reviews

Can an LLM wiki help researchers connect the lessons across reviews?

Project
CS407, Department of Computer Science, University of Warwick
Client
Forensic Capability Network
Supervisor

Why this matters

What a review can tell us

Domestic Homicide Reviews examine the circumstances leading up to a death linked to domestic abuse. They bring together accounts from the agencies involved, including the police, health services and social care.

Each review asks what can be learned to improve safeguarding. It records agency contact, decisions and recommendations for future practice.

NarraTrace works with DHRC-100, a collection of 100 reviews converted from PDF into structured text and checked by researchers.

Looking across cases

A single review tells one case’s story. Comparing reviews can reveal recurring circumstances, gaps in agency responses and lessons that appear again and again.

That comparison takes time. Researchers have to read long reports, find comparable passages and keep track of how the cases differ. In one previous study, 302 documents were collected, but the findings drew on 46.

We are working with the Forensic Capability Network to explore whether language models can help researchers make these comparisons across a larger collection.

Why build a wiki?

A common approach, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), searches for passages relevant to a question and gives them to a language model to produce an answer.

An LLM wiki organises the material before a question is asked. An agent reads the reviews and builds linked pages about people, agencies, events and recommendations. Related information from different reviews can then be explored together.

We will compare the wiki with RAG on the same analysis tasks to test whether those links help, and what they cost to build and use.

A wiki of agencies, events and recommendations

An agency page could bring together the reviews that mention it, the actions it took and the recommendations made to it. Event and relationship pages would offer other routes through the same evidence.

Generated claims will link back to the passages they came from. A separate model will check them against the source, with a sample also checked by people.

The aim is to make evidence across reviews easier to explore, while keeping the original reports available for checking.

How the project works

From reviews to cross-case analysis

The project has four stages: prepare the reviews, build the wiki, analyse patterns and evaluate the results.

1

Corpus preparation

A standardised, hierarchical corpus of reviews

O1

Organise reviews into comparable sections

Rebuild each review’s structure and map its sections to a common report template.

The source data is organised by page. O1 groups the text into sections and subsections, including those that span page breaks, while preserving links to the original content.

Sections will then be mapped to the 2016 statutory guidance template, so corresponding material can be found in the same place across reviews. Heading and text similarity suggest possible matches; an LLM will make the final selection. Sections without a suitable match will be retained.

The database keeps the page-based, hierarchical and standardised versions together. Reviews are also linked to the case characteristics recorded in the Home Office DHR Library. Explore the O1 app.

2

Knowledge organisation

A DHR knowledge base, kept as an LLM wiki

O2

Build linked pages from the reviews

Create a wiki that connects people, agencies, relationships, events and recommendations across the collection.

The wiki will use the prepared review text from Stage 1. Case metadata will be held separately for evaluation, so it cannot supply the wiki with the answers we later test.

An agent will read each review section by section and create or update the relevant pages. Links between pages will connect shared characteristics across cases.

Timelines will preserve the wording of dates alongside a normalised interval and a confidence flag. Explicit links such as “before” and “after” will help retain the order of events. A sample will be checked against manually written chronologies.

3

Analysis

Patterns and insights across reviews

O3

Compare cases, timelines and lessons

Use the wiki to investigate similarities between cases, recurring sequences of events, shared recommendations and the evidence behind case characteristics.

Four tasks will test how useful the wiki is for cross-case analysis. Results will be assessed against the source reviews, with manual checks on a sample.

  1. T1

    Case similarities

    Which reviews are comparable to a given case or profile, and why?

    Why
    Find cases with relevant similarities and understand where their lessons may apply.
    How
    An LLM compares reviews retrieved from the wiki on victim-perpetrator relationship, sequences of behaviour and agency involvement.
    Produces
    A set of reviews, with their similarities and key differences.
    Judged on
    How relevant the chosen reviews are, and how accurate the comparisons.
  2. T2

    Temporal patterns

    Which sequences and timings of behaviour and agency response recur across cases?

    Why
    Examine recurring sequences that may help researchers understand how risk and agency responses develop.
    How
    Compare event sequences and the intervals between them, including patterns whose events are separated by other activity.
    Produces
    The recurring patterns, and how often each appears across reviews.
    Judged on
    Whether the source chronologies support each pattern, and whether the counts are right.
  3. T3

    Recurring recommendations and lessons

    Which recommendations and lessons recur, and which agencies do they concern?

    Why
    Identify recommendations that recur across reviews and the agencies they concern.
    How
    Group recommendations under themes from previous research, and use language models to suggest additional themes.
    Produces
    Themes, how often each recurs, and the agencies each concerns.
    Judged on
    Consistency with the codebook, the right agencies, and the right counts.
  4. T4

    Critical events, guided by case metadata

    Which events back up a case’s recorded characteristics?

    Why
    Connect recorded case characteristics to the events that support them.
    How
    Identify events that support the recorded characteristics, then select a compact set that covers as many of those characteristics as possible.
    Produces
    A compact set of supporting events, linked to the original passages.
    Judged on
    How much of the case metadata a model can rebuild from those events alone.

Case metadata guides the fourth task only. It is excluded from the wiki and RAG index; both approaches receive the same guidance when that task is evaluated.

4

Evaluation

How well it works, and how robust it is

O4

Test what the wiki preserves

Ask a model to reconstruct case narratives from the wiki, then compare them with the original reviews to find omissions, errors and unsupported claims.

For a sample of reviews, a model will write narratives using only the extracted information. We will compare three conditions:

  1. UnmaskedEvery extracted detail is given.
  2. MaskedDemographic, geographic, relational or timing details are left out, one kind at a time.
  3. SubstitutedOne attribute, such as the victim’s ethnicity, is swapped and everything else is fixed.

Each narrative will be checked against the source review fact by fact. Comparing the three conditions will help show how missing or changed details affect what the model writes.

O5

Compare the wiki with RAG

Run the same analysis tasks using the wiki and a RAG baseline, and compare the quality of their results.

Both approaches will use the same source reviews, language model and questions. We will assess each task separately and record token use alongside the quality measures.

O6

Look for characteristics missing from the metadata

Test whether the review text supports case characteristics that are absent from the library’s metadata.

For this test, the model will work without seeing the case metadata:

  1. The wiki agent selects the critical events.
  2. From those events, an LLM proposes tags within the existing case metadata categories.
  3. Reviewers check each tag against the source event it cites, to confirm it is supported.
  4. The tags are compared with the review’s recorded case metadata.
  5. Supported tags missing from the record become candidates to investigate, with a count of reviews for each category.

How we work

We build and test the pipeline in two-week cycles, with regular supervisor reviews.

Test on a sample, then scale up

We will first run a sample of about ten reviews through every stage. This lets us check that the stages work together before processing the full collection.

At the end of each two-week sprint, we review progress with our supervisor, discuss problems and agree the next priorities. Evaluation results feed into that plan.

Every change reviewed

Code is maintained in a shared GitHub repository. Changes go through automated tests and review by another team member before merging.

Parsers are checked against verified outputs, and LLM components use fixed test sets. Windmill runs the processing pipelines; GPU inference is planned for Warwick’s Blythe cluster.

Working with sensitive material

The reviews describe real deaths and the people affected by them. Respect for victims, families and review authors guides how we use and present the material.

Project scope

The project focuses on the Domestic Homicide Reviews in DHRC-100. Its findings may not extend to the broader range of deaths covered by Domestic Abuse Related Death Reviews.

Use of source material

We check each report’s reuse terms and retain attribution to its source.

The reviews contain personal information. We limit processing to the research purpose and check synthetic narratives for close copies of source material.

Our responsibilities

  • Report results honestly and accurately
  • Acknowledge sources and earlier work
  • Handle data lawfully, and only as much as needed
  • Report suspected data breaches or policy breaches promptly
  • Treat victims, their families and review authors with respect

Looking after each other

Reading homicide narratives risks secondary trauma. The team takes breaks, checks in with each other and uses the university’s wellbeing services when needed.

We will reassess ethics requirements if external annotators or experts take part. Generated claims remain unverified until they have been checked against the source material.
