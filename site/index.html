A novel application of LLM wikis to the analysis of Domestic Homicide Reviews

To what extent can an LLM wiki help forensic researchers identify connections and recurring patterns across Domestic Homicide Reviews?

Project
CS407, Department of Computer Science, University of Warwick
Client
Forensic Capability Network
Supervisor
Gabriele Pergola
Why this matters

One review for each death

Since 2011, Community Safety Partnerships in England and Wales have had to commission a Domestic Homicide Review after someone aged 16 or over dies from violence, abuse or neglect by a relative, partner or member of their household.[1]

The agencies involved examine the events that led to the death. The aim is to improve how organisations safeguard people, not to decide who was at fault.

On 15 September 2026 the reviews were renamed Domestic Abuse Related Death Reviews, because they also cover suicides and unexplained deaths linked to domestic abuse.[2]

Patterns only show across many reviews

Each review examines a single death, so failings that keep recurring only come to light once many reviews are compared. The Forensic Capability Network, which coordinates forensic science across the 43 police forces of England and Wales, has typically done that by hand.

Reading by hand is slow and costly: one 2023 report collected 302 documents and drew its results from only 46 of them.[5] Cook et al. name that time and cost as the main bottleneck and propose language processing instead.[6]

Pergola et al. produced DHRC-100: 100 reviews converted from PDF into structured files and checked by people.[7] This project builds on it.

Two ways to question a collection

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) finds the passages that best match a question and hands them to a language model.[3] It suits specific questions, but not ideas spread across a whole collection.

Karpathy’s LLM wiki works differently.[8] An agent writes each document into linked pages as it reads it, so a connection is noted once and reused wherever it applies.

In a small preprint, Cochran found a wiki connected findings better than single-round vector RAG, at about 21 times the tokens per query.[4] This project repeats that comparison on DHRC-100.

A wiki of agencies, events and recommendations

The wiki agent reads each review section by section and writes linked pages about the relationships, events and recommendations in it. An agency page, for example, lists every review that agency appears in, what it did, and the recommendations made to it.

Every claim cites the passages it came from, and a separate model checks each one against its source before the wiki is used.[13]

As far as the team knows, this is the first time an agent-maintained wiki has been applied to these reviews.

How the project works

Four stages, six objectives

Each stage works from what the stage before it produced. Stages 2 and 3 are the heart of the research.

1

Corpus preparation

A standardised, hierarchical corpus of reviews

O1

Derive a hierarchical, standardised representation of DHRC-100

Organise DHRC-100 by each review’s own structure, standardise it against the 2016 statutory guidance template, and store the result in a database linked to the case metadata in the Home Office DHR Library.

DHRC-100 stores each review page by page. Using its checked headings and the sections they belong to, the content is organised by the document’s own hierarchy, with each section’s text grouped across page breaks. The original content, source references and layout are kept, so later stages can retrieve a section with everything nested under it. The result is intended for release in the DHRC-100 repository.

A second, standardised form maps each section onto the overview report template in the 2016 guidance,[14] so the same material can be found in the same place in every review. Candidate template sections are found by heading, exact or fuzzy, and by meaning, using embeddings. An LLM then chooses one, following the candidate and re-ranking approach of Liu et al.[15] A section with no suitable match is kept and flagged as unmatched.

All three forms go into one database, and each review is linked to its case metadata: the characteristics recorded alongside it in the DHR Library.[16]

2

Knowledge organisation

A DHR knowledge base, kept as an LLM wiki

O2

Construct an LLM wiki DHR knowledge base

Develop an agent-maintained LLM wiki over the DHRC-100 database that organises entities, events and recommendations into persistent, linked pages.

The wiki is built from the Stage 1 database, which stays read-only so the original evidence is preserved. The wiki agent never reads the case metadata: that is kept back to evaluate the later objectives.

The agent works through each review section by section, recording relationships, events and recommendations, and organises them into linked pages. Relationship and event pages connect reviews that share characteristics, which is what the analysis in Stage 3 relies on.

The order of events matters: the lead-up to a death often involves several agencies and a sequence of contacts and decisions. LLMs remain weaker than people at temporal reasoning and putting events in order,[17][18] so event pages store dates three ways, and relations such as before, after or overlapping are stored explicitly.[19] The overall order is worked out in code, not by the LLM, and a sample of timelines is checked against chronologies written by hand.

Before the wiki is used, a separate LLM judge checks every generated claim against the Stage 1 passages it cites. The judge is a different model from the wiki agent, to reduce the bias of models favouring their own output,[13] and its verdicts are checked against a sample of claims labelled by hand.[12]

3

Analysis

Patterns and insights across reviews

O3

Identify case similarities, temporal patterns, recurring lessons and critical events across DHRs

Use the knowledge base to compare case circumstances, analyse patterns over time in behaviour and agency responses, find recurring recommendations and lessons, and extract critical events.

The wiki supports four analysis tasks. An LLM judge assesses the results of the first three against the source material, and a sample of its judgements is checked by hand.

  1. T1

    Case similarities

    Which reviews are comparable to a given case or profile, and why?

    Why
    To judge whether lessons from comparable cases apply, drawing on case-based reasoning.[20]
    How
    An LLM compares reviews retrieved from the wiki on victim-perpetrator relationship, sequences of behaviour and agency involvement.
    Produces
    A set of reviews, with their similarities and key differences.
    Judged on
    How relevant the chosen reviews are, and how accurate the comparisons.
  2. T2

    Temporal patterns

    Which sequences and timings of behaviour and agency response recur across cases?

    Why
    To find chances to recognise risk and respond, drawing on Monckton-Smith’s temporal analysis.[21]
    How
    Sequential pattern mining finds orders of events that recur across timelines, even when the events aren’t consecutive.[22] The intervals between them are then compared.
    Produces
    The recurring patterns, and how often each appears across reviews.
    Judged on
    Whether the source chronologies support each pattern, and whether the counts are right.
  3. T3

    Recurring recommendations and lessons

    Which recommendations and lessons recur, and which agencies do they concern?

    Why
    To find areas for improvement that agencies share, drawing on thematic analysis of DHR recommendations.[5]
    How
    A codebook from the literature sets the starting themes.[23] LLM topic modelling sorts material into them and proposes new ones.[24]
    Produces
    Themes, how often each recurs, and the agencies each concerns.
    Judged on
    Consistency with the codebook, the right agencies, and the right counts.
  4. T4

    Critical events, guided by case metadata

    Which events back up a case’s recorded characteristics?

    Why
    To make the evidence for each recorded characteristic easy to inspect, following earlier work on critical events.[7]
    How
    An LLM looks for events in the wiki that support the case metadata, then greedy set cover keeps the smallest set that still covers it.
    Produces
    The smallest set of events from which the case metadata can be rebuilt.
    Judged on
    How much of the case metadata a model can rebuild from those events alone.[7]

Case metadata is only an input to T4. It is kept out of the wiki and the RAG index, and in T4 the wiki and RAG get the same guidance.

4

Evaluation

How well it works, and how robust it is

O4

Investigate model comprehension through synthetic reconstruction

Generate case narratives from structured review information to measure what is lost, how much depends on context, and where bias may lie, by hiding details in a controlled way.

For each review in a random sample, an LLM writes a case narrative from the parts Stage 2 extracted, without seeing the source text. It does this three times:

  1. UnmaskedEvery extracted detail is given.
  2. MaskedDemographic, geographic, relational or timing details are left out, one kind at a time.
  3. SubstitutedOne attribute, such as the victim’s ethnicity, is swapped and everything else is fixed.[25][26]

Each narrative is compared with the source review one atomic fact at a time,[11] to see what the model keeps, leaves out or invents, and how that changes with the information it has. The LLM judges this needs have known weaknesses: their verdicts can vary on the same task, and they tend to favour text they generated themselves.[12][13]

O5

Evaluate analytical performance

Compare the wiki with a RAG baseline over the source documents on the Stage 3 tasks, measuring the difference task by task.

The four tasks run twice, once with the wiki agent and once with conventional RAG. Following Cochran, a single-round vector RAG runs first, with stronger variants tested later.[4] Both use the same Stage 1 corpus, LLM and questions, so any difference reflects how knowledge is organised and retrieved, and how many tokens each uses.

O6

Investigate inferred case characteristics through label-free critical event extraction

Find characteristics the reviews support but their case metadata leaves out, and count the reviews affected in each existing category.

Unlike T4, the model is never given the review’s case metadata. For each review:

  1. The wiki agent selects the critical events.
  2. From those events, an LLM proposes tags within the existing case metadata categories.
  3. Reviewers check each tag against the source event it cites, to confirm it is supported.
  4. The tags are compared with the review’s recorded case metadata.
  5. Supported tags missing from the record become candidates to investigate, with a count of reviews for each category.

How we work

Evidence decides what gets built, and short cycles keep the project on schedule.

Build, test, learn, in two-week sprints

The project pairs Design Science Research, which builds and evaluates artefacts that address a problem,[27] with Agile development.[28] What each evaluation finds can change what is built next.

Sprints, adapted from Scrum,[29] last two weeks and end in a meeting with the supervisor to review progress, deal with problems and set priorities. Following Lean principles,[30] about ten reviews go through the whole pipeline before it runs on all 100, so problems joining the stages together show up early.

Every change reviewed

The team works in one shared GitHub repository, on short-lived branches. A pull request needs passing automated tests and approval from another member before it merges.[31]

Fixed parts such as parsers are checked against outputs already verified;[32] LLM parts are tested on fixed test sets. Pipeline scripts reach a self-hosted Windmill server from Git, and models run on Warwick’s Blythe GPU cluster.

Handled with care

These are accounts of real deaths. The team treats victims, their families and the reviews’ authors with respect, and never contacts victims’ families.

Only Domestic Homicide Reviews

DHRC-100 and the DHR Library hold only Domestic Homicide Reviews, so the newer Domestic Abuse Related Death Reviews are out of scope. Findings may not carry over to the wider range of deaths those reviews cover.[2]

The law

The reviews are published under the Open Government Licence v3.0, which allows reuse with attribution.[37] Each report’s terms are checked against its Community Safety Partnership’s guidelines.

They contain personal information, so processing follows the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018,[38] with public task as the lawful basis and the research safeguards of Article 89. Synthetic data is checked for near copies of real reviews.

Our responsibilities

  • Report results honestly and accurately
  • Acknowledge sources and earlier work
  • Handle data lawfully, and only as much as needed
  • Report suspected data breaches or policy breaches promptly
  • Treat victims, their families and review authors with respect

Looking after each other

Reading homicide narratives risks secondary trauma. The team takes breaks, checks in with each other and uses the university’s wellbeing services when needed.

Analysing published documents doesn’t need formal ethical review;[39] the team will check again if outside annotators or experts label data. Every claim an LLM makes is treated as unverified until it has been checked, in the wiki and in any other work an agent helps with.
